Stage 3 · The Carbon-14 Clock

A Clock Hidden in Nature

Did you know that every living thing contains carbon?

Some of that carbon is a special kind called carbon-14.

Plants take in carbon from their surroundings. Animals get carbon by eating plants or other animals.

As long as something is alive, it continues taking in carbon.

Living organism — taking in carbon

Stage 3 · The Carbon-14 Clock

The Clock Keeps Ticking

When a living thing dies, it stops taking in new carbon.

The carbon-14 already inside it continues to disappear, little by little.

Scientists can measure how much carbon-14 remains to help estimate how long ago it died.

That's radiocarbon dating!

More carbon-14 remaining usually means less time has passed since the organism died. Less carbon-14 remaining usually means more time has passed.

This is a simplified scientific model. Real radiocarbon dates involve careful sampling, measurement, and calibration.

⌕ Investigated Using Other Methods

What did we learn?

  1. Material matters.Radiocarbon dating works by measuring carbon-14 in suitable materials, especially those that came from living organisms.
  2. Every method has limits.Even when something was once alive, it might be too old or too poorly preserved for radiocarbon dating.
  3. Archaeologists use many kinds of evidence.When radiocarbon dating isn't possible, archaeologists use archaeological context and other scientific methods to investigate the past.

Final question

One Final Mystery

During your excavation, you discover a stone tool and a piece of charcoal buried in the same archaeological layer.

You know that you cannot radiocarbon date the stone tool itself.

But could dating the charcoal help you investigate when the tool might have been used?

Archaeologists don't just study individual objects. They also investigate where objects were found and how they relate to the evidence around them.

That's what makes archaeology so fascinating!

Archaeologist's notebook

Your Field Notes

Radiocarbon dating helps scientists estimate how long ago suitable materials stopped exchanging carbon with their environment.

Before sending a discovery to the laboratory, ask yourself:

  • What is this object made from?
  • Did its material come from something living?
  • Could it be too old for radiocarbon dating?
